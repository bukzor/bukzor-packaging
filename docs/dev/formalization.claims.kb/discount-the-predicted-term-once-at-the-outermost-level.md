# Discount the Predicted Term, Once, at the Outermost Level

Carrier: an estimate mixing recorded quantities with forecast ones. Laws:

> **Placement.** The discount attaches to the **predicted** term and never to the
> measured one. Ongoing activity is recorded, not predicted -- a thing being done
> weekly right now is evidence.
>
> **Multiplicity.** Probabilities *inside* the model are not discounts, so a
> model containing a P(needed) and an outer coefficient is not double-counting.
> But a discount on a discount has no natural floor, so the number of levels is
> settled by fiat: **one**.

## The consequence that makes it worth stating

The same coefficient gates both directions of a system's size. It **raises** the
bar to build -- a forecast-only justification needs 1/coefficient times the
benefit -- and it **lowers** the bar to remove, because the benefit forfeited by
removing is mostly forecast. One coefficient, two gates, one direction:
**distrusting predictions is monotone pressure toward less.**

This is easy to miss because the two gates are usually written by different people
in different documents, and each looks like an isolated preference for caution.

## Smallest instance

A benefit model with a P(needed again) inside it, under an outer ⅔-off
coefficient. It looks like the same uncertainty priced twice, and it is legal
exactly because one factor is part of the estimate and the other corrects the
estimator. Nobody had said so, and the unstated version had a real cost: the
effective bar for the class of things justified by future need was far higher than
the coefficient suggested.

## What would kill it

A class of predictions that turn out calibrated. The coefficient should then rise
toward 1 for that class, and the pressure disappears with it. Overturning it
requires a record of past predictions and how they landed -- and the absence of
such a record is itself an argument for keeping the discount steep.
