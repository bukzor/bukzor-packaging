# An Exchange Rate Is Elicited From Past Decisions, Not Measured

Carrier: two terms of a comparison in incommensurable units, and the decisions
already made between them. Operation: read each past decision as a constraint on
the rate λ that would convert one unit into the other. Law:

> Each decision is a **half-plane** on λ. The feasible λ is their intersection --
> an interval. It narrows with every non-degenerate decision, and **an empty
> intersection refutes linearity rather than refuting the decisions.**

So a gate whose two terms have no common unit is not blocked. It is waiting on two
hard cases, and it pays for itself: every decision made under it makes the next one
cheaper.

## Degenerate decisions carve nothing

A decision in which one term is **zero** is satisfied by every λ > 0. It feels like
progress and teaches nothing.

**Smallest instance:** exactly one retirement had ever been made in the subject
ledger, and its forfeited benefit was zero -- the easiest possible case, and
therefore the one that constrained nothing. The feasible interval is still
(0, ∞) after it.

That generalizes: the cases that are obvious are the cases that carry no
information about your own tradeoffs. If you want the rate, you have to decide
something that was close.

## Measure the half you can

The two terms are rarely equally observable. A cost already being paid leaves a
record; a benefit is a counterfactual against a world where the thing does not
exist, and no log contains it. **Measure the observable side in its own units and
elicit only the rate**, which is one number in units a human can actually judge.
Two calibrations become one.

## What would kill it

A third factor outside the two terms -- taste, embarrassment, an obligation to
someone else. The intersection comes back empty, which is not a failure of the
method but its result: the decisions are not explained by any tradeoff between the
terms you named.
